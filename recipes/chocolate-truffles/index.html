What a truffle is

The chocolate truffle is named after the black Périgord truffle, which it resembles: small, matte and irregular. It appeared in France in the late 19th or early 20th century as a ganache centre, portioned and rolled by hand, finished in a thin chocolate shell or cocoa powder.

  • A handmade truffle is defined by handwork, controlled irregularity and textural contrast, not by novelty flavours or polish.
  • Later commercial versions moved towards thick shells and decoration. This is the opposite of the form.
  • It is chocolate work, not a dairy preparation, and there is nowhere to hide errors.

The target

  • Size: 10 to 14 g finished. Eaten in one or two bites; anything larger fails the form.
  • Texture: dense and soft, with no ooze and no crumble.
  • Bite: gives immediately and cuts cleanly.
  • Taste: chocolate first, with dairy and fat supporting it, not masking it.
  • Appearance: matte interior, uniform colour, no fat bloom.
  • No grain, no waxiness, and a shell you barely notice.

Ganache and ratios

Ganache is an emulsion of fat and water, held together by the lecithin in the chocolate and the dairy. Think of it as chocolate hydrated by cream, not as cream with chocolate added.

  • Write the ratio with chocolate as 100% and everything else as a percentage of it.
    • Chocolate is the structure: cocoa butter crystallising sets firmness, snap and melt, and the cocoa solids set viscosity and body.
    • Chocolate is the thing being hydrated, and the thing you most often change.
    • The useful question is "how much liquid and fat can this chocolate take?"
  • Cream does not form structure. It loosens the fat network that the chocolate builds. A ratio with cream at 100% describes a sauce or a glaze, and cannot explain why two chocolates behave differently at the same ratio.
  • More dairy means a softer ganache.
  • Too fluid: it sets weak and slumps when rolled. Too viscous: dense in the mouth, with a muted melt.
  • To change the flavour, raise the chocolate percentage or change the chocolate, not the ratio.

The base formula: 1 to 1

Equal weights of chocolate and cream. This is the one to learn first.

  • It assumes:
    • dark chocolate of at least 60 to 66%
    • heavy (double) cream and nothing else: no invert sugar, glucose or butter
    • it sets at room temperature. If it needs the fridge, something else is wrong.
  • For: an easy ratio to nudge firmer or softer, few ingredients, few decisions and few ways to fail. It teaches handling.
  • Against: basic texture and flavour. It is not the softest centre or the longest shelf life.
  • Common errors:
    • cream boiled too long, so water evaporates and the ratio shifts
    • cream too cold or too hot
    • chocolate not fully melted
    • overmixing
    • not resting it long enough
  • When it "doesn't work", it is usually one of these:
    • chocolate chips were used instead of chocolate
    • the kitchen was too warm
    • no rest between steps
    • the ratio was adjusted on the fly

Caramelised chocolate ganache

A professional formula built on caramelised sugar and glucose, cream, butter, salt and a 61% chocolate. The notes this page is drawn from describe how it behaves but give no quantities.

  • The softer version sits near the upper limit of softness for a truffle. It chooses flavour and mouthfeel over forgiveness.
    • For: an ethereal texture and a complex flavour.
    • Against: it demands speed and practised handling. There is more free water, the extra fat speeds the melt, and the caramel has a narrow working window.
  • It is balanced by:
    • the type and concentration of sugar, which lowers water activity
    • how far the caramel is taken, which sets the bitterness
    • the chocolate, whose flavour stays dominant
    • butter used for texture, not as the main flavour
    • where the salt goes
  • The firmer version reduces the cream and butter and leaves the sugar, salt and chocolate alone. The flavour change is below what you can taste; what changes is the margin for error.
    • It scoops cleanly with less smearing and holds a sphere for longer while rolling.
    • It tolerates hands that are a few degrees warmer.
    • It still melts at once, and is slightly more elastic at room temperature.

The shell

A thin membrane, not armour. Too thick and it overwhelms the filling; too thin and it fails in handling.

  • Use tempered chocolate with high fluidity, from the same family as the filling but with slightly more cocoa butter.
  • As it sets it contracts around the centre, sealing it and keeping moisture in.
  • Two thin coats by hand: the first is a primer, the second is the structure.
  • Aim for a thin, continuous shell of even thickness, a clean snap that does not shatter, and no pool at the base.
  • Tempering by the direct method:
    • Start with chocolate that is already in temper.
    • Melt it gently, only most of the way (to about 80%), and never past the maximum temperature for that type of chocolate.
    • Do not let it get too cold while you work, and do not over-agitate it, which over-crystallises it.
    • Add more good chocolate to hold the working temperature.
  • Cocoa powder can go on the bare ganache or over the shell. It balances the sweetness, protects the shell from fingerprints and moisture, and softens visual flaws.

The process

Every stage ends with a rest. Time is the control, not cold.

  • Make the ganache. Rest.
  • Portion with a disher, all the same size. Rest.
    • If it smears or strings, it is too soft or too warm. Stop and rest it.
  • Roll into shape with gloved hands, quickly. Rest.
    • A light tack is fine. Sticking is not.
    • You should feel resistance first and softening second.
    • The seam should disappear. If it does not, the emulsion is poor.
    • Shape, do not knead. If it flows it is a sauce; if it cracks it is under-hydrated.
    • Afterwards it should hold its dome without slumping, and the surface should relax, not sweat.
  • Coat twice in tempered chocolate, with the centres at room temperature, not cold. Rest.
    • The first coat should cling, not slide. If it slides, the centre is too soft or the chocolate is not fluid enough.
    • Each shell should set while you work through the rest of the tray, and set without streaks.
    • The second coat seals. It should not add thickness.
    • Move finished truffles to clean parchment. They should feel light for their size.
  • Dredge in cocoa powder. Sift it off to serve, or leave the truffles in it for storage.
  • Stop at once if you catch yourself thinking any of these. They are excuses, not observations:
    • "The coating will fix it."
    • "One more roll will fix it."
    • "It will firm up later."
    • "This batch is just soft."

Choosing chocolate

  • Use couverture, which has at least 31% cocoa butter. Not chocolate chips, baking chips, melting wafers, candy coating, compound chocolate or ordinary eating bars.
  • On the packet, look for:
    • the word couverture, or chocolate sold as callets, fèves or pistoles
    • 60 to 70% for the ganache, and the same or slightly higher for the coating
    • an ingredient list that starts with cocoa mass and cocoa butter
  • Put it back if it says:
    • "no tempering needed", "great for melting" or "chocolate flavoured"
    • candy melts
    • palm, coconut or soy oil in the ingredients
  • Ignore origin stories, tasting notes and words like artisan, luxury and premium.
  • The cocoa percentage is total cocoa content, solids plus cocoa butter. It tells you about flavour intensity, not about how the chocolate flows.
  • Fluidity is often shown as drops on the packet or the maker's website. Two drops is low fluidity and gives a thicker shell. Four drops is high fluidity and gives a thin one, which is what double coating wants.
  • For the ganache:
    • 60 to 66%: easiest to work with
    • 67 to 70%: firmer and more intense
    • over 70%: difficult
  • For the coating:
    • 54 to 66%: a thinner shell and easier dipping
    • 67 to 70%: a stronger snap, and it needs better tempering
    • milk chocolate has a narrow temper window and is harder for beginners
  • Brands named in the notes:
    • Valrhona: Caraïbe 66% for ganache, Guanaja 70% for coating
    • Cacao Barry: 60 to 70% callets, the most forgiving and good for learning
    • Guittard: 61 to 63% wafers, clean flavour and stable behaviour
    • Ghirardelli Intense Dark 60% bars: a supermarket fallback, acceptable for ganache if chopped

Storage

  • Keep at 15 to 18°C in low humidity. Set ganache at 18 to 21°C.
  • Do not refrigerate the centres or the finished truffles.
    • Cold makes fats contract fast and crystallise unevenly, in unstable forms. That leads to bloom, streaks and a poor snap.
    • Cold ganache feels firm but is not stronger. Once it warms it softens unevenly and the hidden faults appear, often during rolling and dipping.
    • Humidity causes condensation and sugar bloom, and shortens shelf life sharply.
    • If it needs the fridge to behave, it will not behave later.
  • Store in breathable paper or trays, not airtight plastic, with space between truffles so they do not rub.
  • Cocoa powder stops them sticking to the tray and helps them keep.
  • They keep for 2 to 3 weeks.

Judging the result

  • Cut one in half, listen for the snap, and check for bloom after 24 to 48 hours.
  • Flavour should move from shell to ganache to finish, and the truffle should disappear cleanly, with no lingering wax or heaviness.
  • The coating should show no streaks, drag marks or air bubbles, and the base should be sealed without a foot.
  • Success: consistent across batches, irregular on purpose, stable at room temperature, and a chocolate you can identify.
  • Failure: a thick shell, a greasy interior, bloom within days, uneven sizes, or overworked ganache that is separated, grainy or elastic.

Flavour ideas

  • Ganache: lychee, coconut, banana, sesame, peppermint, malted milk, lavender, lemongrass, green tea, Earl Grey, pumpkin, sweet potato, pistachio, hazelnut, peanut butter, vanilla, caramel, coffee, rum, Cointreau, Grand Marnier, stout.
  • Coating: white, milk or dark chocolate, or a flavoured couverture such as Dulcey, Caramelia, raspberry, yuzu or passion fruit.
  • Décor: vermicelli, chocolate pearls, nonpareils, chocolate curls.

Notes

  • Rest between every step, and let time, not cold, do the work.
  • Never use chocolate chips: their stabilisers prevent a proper melt and temper.
  • Warm hands overwork ganache. Work quickly, and cool your hands on an ice pack if you need to.
